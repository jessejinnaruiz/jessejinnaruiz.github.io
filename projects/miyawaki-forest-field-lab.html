A practical field guide, not a recipe

Grow a tiny forest.

A Miyawaki-inspired project notebook for organizers: learn the design questions, shape a first site plan, and see what still needs local expertise.

Evidence, not hype. Dense native planting is a design approach, not a guaranteed shortcut to a mature, self-sustaining forest. Published studies vary in methods and follow-up. Start with local ecology, site conditions, and maintenance capacity.

Before you put a spade in the ground

Use these prompts to assemble a responsible project brief. Species, spacing, soil amendments, permits, and water needs must be selected for the actual site with qualified local guidance.

01 / Know the place

Record the site history, slope, drainage, sunlight, soil texture, compaction, utilities, access, and nearby habitat. Get permission and locate underground utilities before excavation.

02 / Define the reference ecosystem

Identify the locally appropriate native plant community for this soil and landform. A nearby park is not automatically the right reference. Consult an ecologist, native-plant specialist, or regional conservation group.

03 / Build a diverse palette

Document candidate canopy, sub-canopy, shrub, and ground-layer species, their provenance, mature size, water needs, and availability. Do not treat a generic species list as a Central Texas prescription.

04 / Design density from evidence

Published Miyawaki-style projects use dense planting, but exact spacing depends on species, stock, site, climate, and care. Ask the project ecologist for a written spacing rationale before estimating stock.

05 / Prepare the soil carefully

Use a soil test and document how the proposed preparation addresses compaction, drainage, contamination risk, and organic matter. Avoid blanket amendment recipes; they can be wrong for the site.

06 / Budget for survival

Budget beyond planting day: watering, mulch replenishment, invasive control, replacement plants, volunteer coordination, accessibility, and monitoring. Assign an owner for each task and season.

07 / Agree on stewardship

Name the landowner, project lead, watering team, escalation contact, and long-term maintenance partner. Record access rights and what happens if the original organizer leaves.

08 / Measure what happens

Choose repeatable measures such as survival by species, canopy cover, volunteer hours, and maintenance effort. Publish the baseline, method, and follow-up dates; do not promise biodiversity or carbon outcomes in advance.
